# Abstract

**Background**: Zimbabwe’s national guidelines for STI management recommend that high-risk women presenting with vaginal discharge syndrome (VDS) are prescribed antibiotics for gonorrhea (NG), chlamydia (CT), trichomoniasis (TV), and bacterial vaginosis (BV). The performance of this approach depends on its clinical interpretation and implementation. Here, we investigate the potential relative impact of an NG/CT/TV point-of-care (POC) test on undertreatment, overtreatment and disease burden, in the context of different implementations of syndromic management of women with VDS.

**Methods**: We created an agent-based model with an age-risk-stratified sexual network and modeled co-circulation of NG, CT, and TV along with HIV and BV. We estimated symptomatic proportions and care-seeking rates under three different scenarios around the implementation of treatment guidelines, corresponding to all, most, or half of women being treated for NG+CT upon presentation with VDS. For each implementation scenario we estimated disease burden and over/undertreatment rates assuming continuation of the standard of care with/without a POC NG/CT/TV test available over 2027-2040.

**Results**: Under a treat-all interpretation of the syndromic management guidelines, we estimate that 70-80% of antibiotics for NG/CT would currently be given to women without these infections. Overtreatment would fall to \<5% if a sensitive POC test for NG/CT/TV were available. However, if the implementation of the guidelines implies that only half of women seeking care for VDS are treated, then a POC test would also reduce undertreatment and disease burden, with \>500k additional women correctly treated for NG and \~1.5m correctly treated for CT and TV, and 24%/15% reductions in the number of women with NG/CT by 2040\.

**Conclusion**: Improved data on the functioning of syndromic management in practice would help refine the estimates of the health impact and the overall value proposition of a highly sensitive POC diagnostic for NG/CT/TV. However, even without such data, our analysis demonstrates the potential for such a diagnostic to reduce overtreatment by \>90% relative to plausible assumptions regarding the standard of care.

# Research in context

**What is already known on this topic** Syndromic management of vaginal discharge syndrome is the standard of care in many low- and middle-income countries but is known to lead to substantial overtreatment. Point-of-care diagnostics for sexually transmitted infections have been identified as a potential strategy to improve treatment appropriateness.

**What this study adds** Using an agent-based model calibrated to Zimbabwe, we demonstrate that universal implementation of point-of-care testing could reduce overtreatment for gonorrhoea and chlamydia by 85-95% compared to syndromic management. 

**How this study might affect research, practice or policy** Our findings emphasize the critical importance of understanding how well syndromic management is functioning in practice: if all women with vaginal discharge receive appropriate treatment, point-of-care diagnostics offer minimal immediate health benefits, whereas if syndromic management is implemented imperfectly, point-of-care testing substantially reduces both undertreatment and disease burden. This demonstrates that accurate assessment of current standard-of-care performance is essential for evaluating the potential value of point-of-care diagnostics and informing investment decisions in resource-limited settings.

# Introduction

In high- and middle-income countries, vaginal discharge syndrome (VDS) ranks among the top ten reasons for women of reproductive age to seek clinical care \[1–3\]. A systematic review from 2022 found no comparable data from low-income countries \[2\], but given the high incidence of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) across Sub-Saharan Africa, the Americas, and South-East Asia \[4–7\], it is expected that a sizable share of healthcare facility capacity across these regions is dedicated to the management of VDS. Establishing efficient, effective, and scalable case management of VDS is crucial in these resource-limited settings. To meet this need, the World Health Organisation (WHO) first developed syndromic management guidelines in 1984 \[1\], which recommended treating individuals presenting with VDS with a combination of antibiotics covering the most common causes without requiring an individual etiological diagnosis. Four decades after these guidelines were first published, syndromic management remains the standard of care across Southern Africa. In more recent years, WHO guidelines have been updated to recommend optimization of syndromic management by integrating etiological diagnosis where possible \[1\], but this remains rare.

There are well-documented benefits to the syndromic management approach: it is often lower-cost than reliance on laboratory-based assays, and it covers women with multiple etiologies \[1\]. However, as syndromic treatment typically involves multiple antibiotics to cover the diverse potential aetiology of VDS, many women receive one or more of these antibiotics unnecessarily. The overprescription of antibiotics is a cause for concern in the context of increasing global levels of antibiotic resistance, particularly for gonorrhea. Point-of-care (POC) diagnostics capable of detecting *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* (NG), *Chlamydia trachomatis* (CT), and *Trichomonas vaginalis* (TV) – the three STIs most commonly associated with VDS – could potentially reduce the overuse of antibiotics among symptomatic women. 

VDS management guidelines can range from a treat-all approach (where treatments for NG+CT+TV as well as bacterial vaginosis (BV) are prescribed to all women with VDS) to more conservative implementations where treatment decisions are made based on physical examination and risk assessment, wherein the proportion treated might be considerably lower and the risk of undertreatment higher. In addition, although in most countries these guidelines are determined at a national level, their interpretation and implementation is likely to vary in practice, given the potentially subjective nature of risk assessments and examinations with/without speculum, as well as sometimes limited clinical training and the impact of drug stockouts \[8\]. As such, an assessment of the impact and value of a POC diagnostic for NG+CT+TV is likely to be strongly dependent on the implementation of the standard of care (SOC) to which it is added. 

This study builds on prior literature evaluating the impact and acceptability of POC STI diagnostics \[9,10\], with a focus on the value of understanding how the standard of care is operating in practice. For our analysis we focused on Zimbabwe, which has a relatively high burden of HIV (adult prevalence is estimated at around 10%), and where nearly a quarter of young women aged 16-24 had at least one STI out of CT, NG, or TV in 2020 \[9,11,12\]. We developed a model of STI transmission in Zimbabwe and used this to estimate the effect of a POC diagnostic for NG+CT+TV on overtreatment, undertreatment, and burden of disease, under diverse SOC implementation scenarios.

# Methods

We developed a new agent-based microsimulator called STIsim, capable of simulating multiple co-infecting STIs plus HIV transmitting across age- and risk-structured sexual networks \[13\]. STIsim leverages the Starsim disease modeling platform \[13\]. We used STIsim to define a population of agents representative of the population of Zimbabwe in 1990, and used UN estimates of age-specific fertility, age- and sex-disaggregated mortality, and overall population size to project births and deaths over 1990–2025 \[14\]. We created an age- and risk-stratified sexual network for agent interactions, with the population divided into three risk groups representing people who marry and do not have extramarital relationships, people who marry and divorce and/or have extramarital relationships, and people who never marry. In addition, we modeled transactional sex, with lifetime participation rates of 5% for women and 20% for men. Our model currently only represents heterosexual partnerships. The sexual network is described in the [Supplementary Materials](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1O-QZv0YQYsozZR1dBpkn-gpHEWTxfyC8Ieo09nGMa0g/edit?usp=sharing).

## Disease dynamics

We simulate three STIs which cause VDS: NG, CT, and TV. People can have multiple infections simultaneously, although we do not capture super-infection (e.g. people with a current NG infection can acquire CT or TV, but not a concurrent NG infection). Some infected people develop symptoms, with symptomaticity and duration varying by pathogen, sex, and individual (Table S1). We assume that NG and CT infections clear in the absence of treatment, but some TV infections persist in women (Table S1). We do not explicitly model symptoms of STI infection at other sites (e.g. anorectal discharge). We additionally model HIV, primarily to assist with model calibration; a description of our HIV model is in the Supplementary Materials.

Since the purpose of this analysis is to evaluate methods for the management of women presenting with vaginal discharge, we also capture vaginal discharge attributable to bacterial vaginosis (BV). We assume prevalence of BV of 40% \[15,16\], with 10% of cases leading to care-seeking for abnormal vaginal discharge annually. 

## Syndromic management scenarios

Zimbabwe’s National STI Management Guidelines outline steps for treating people presenting with VDS \[17\], following the WHO’s risk-based algorithm (Figure S1). Although the sensitivity and specificity of syndromic management have been extensively reviewed \[1,18,19\], it is difficult to synthesize evidence across studies/settings since the sensitivity and specificity of the algorithm depends on the true prevalence of STIs among those seeking care. Performance estimates frequently refer to the algorithms’ performance among combined cohorts of asymptomatic and symptomatic women. Furthermore, evaluation of recommended algorithms may not reflect true implementation where clinician interpretation and adherence to guidelines can vary widely \[8\]. Therefore, rather than anchoring our analysis on a single assumption, we consider three alternative SOC scenarios, as outlined in Figure 1a. The “treat-all” scenario represents an upper limit on the extent of treatment given: all women presenting with VDS would be treated for NG+CT+TV/BV, implying sensitivity of 100% and specificity of 0%. The “treat-most” scenario represents our best interpretation of how Zimbabwe’s VDS management guidelines are likely to operate. For this scenario, we assume that NG/CT treatments would be given to 70% of those with NG or CT infection and 50% of those without. Finally, the “treat-half” scenario represents a more pessimistic view of the algorithm’s performance; here we assume that treatment for NG+CT will be given to 50% of women regardless of their true infection status, i.e. that screening on the basis of risk assessment and physical examination has sensitivity/specificity of 50/50. This scenario is comparable to the estimates in a study by Cornier et al \[19\], that focuses on a cohort of women with VDS which reported performance estimates of 60/60.

Figure 1b illustrates our assumptions for how the three SOC implementation scenarios would be augmented by the addition of a POC diagnostic for NG/CT/TV. We assume a POC diagnostic with sensitivity of 98% and specificity of 95% for symptomatic women, available from 2027\. Under all scenarios, we assume that treatment for NG/CT/TV will be given on the basis of the test result. We assume all women would be treated only for any STIs identified by the test result, and that 100%/80%/50% of women would be treated for BV in the treat-all/most/half scenarios.

## Model calibration and analysis

Model calibration is described in the Supplementary Materials. Our analyses focus on three core outcomes: 1\) the extent of overtreatment, i.e. administration of antibiotics to treat infections that women do not have, 2\) the extent of undertreatment, i.e. care-seeking women not treated for infections they do have, and 3\) the disease burden. For each of the implementation scenarios, we estimate these outcome measures over 2027–2040, firstly assuming continuation of the standard of care and secondly assuming a highly sensitive POC diagnostic was available starting in 2027\.

# Results

Figure 2 presents core epidemiological results from the treat-most implementation scenario. Our model estimates indicate that transmission of all three STIs is largely driven by high-risk sexual behavior, especially transactional sex, which contributes around 80% of NG transmissions, 62% of CT infections, and 50% of TV transmissions over 2000-2020 in Zimbabwe. Prevalence of NG is highest in 25-30 year old women, peaking at \~3.5%, while CT prevalence peaks at around 14%, mirroring empirical data \[11\]. The epidemiology of TV differs substantially due to the persistence of asymptomatic infection in women, which implies higher prevalence in women aged 30 and above. Overall, prevalence of vaginal discharge attributable to all 3 symptomatic STIs plus BV is estimated at around 10%. These epidemiologic characteristics are comparable in the ‘treat-all’ and ‘treat-half’ scenarios.

Figure 3 highlights the interdependence between the three implementation scenarios and the parameters estimated during calibration. For each implementation scenario, we estimate the symptomatic share of cases for each STI, plus the proportion of women experiencing VDS who seek care, which, when combined with the implementation scenario, give an overall estimate of the share of cases in women that are treated. These three metrics are plotted in the top row of Figure 3, while the bottom row shows the overall estimated prevalence for each STI (a key calibration target). Figure 3a shows the mean symptomatic share across the 500 best-fitting simulations, which ranges between 10-19% for NG, 26-28% for CT, and 65-72% for TV, depending on the scenario. Figure 3b highlights that there is an inverse relationship between the estimated proportion of women experiencing VDS who seek care, and the assumed proportion of these women who are treated. Intuitively, the more treatment we assume, the lower the implied proportion of women who seek care must be, in order to end up with approximately equivalent chances of being treated across scenarios (Figure 3c) and therefore approximately equivalent estimates of STI prevalence (Figures 3d–f).

Next, we investigate the degree of overtreatment under each implementation scenario, and the effect of a POC diagnostic. Core results on overtreatment, undertreatment, and disease burden are summarized in Table S12, which presents the mean results aggregated over the 500 best-fitting parameter sets, and in Figure 4, which shows these same results with uncertainty intervals and over time.

Figures 4a and 4c show that in all implementation scenarios, a POC diagnostic results in substantial reductions in overtreatment over 2027–2040, reaching estimated declines of 85–95% in overtreatment for NG and CT (Figure 4c, Table S12). The reduction in overtreatment for TV is much smaller: on the assumption that the POC diagnostic would be embedded within the syndromic management algorithm, and that women who tested negative to all STIs included in the assay would nevertheless be prescribed metronidazole for presumed BV infection, we would not anticipate similar declines in TV overtreatment.  For all drug regimens, levels of overtreatment are highest in the treat-all scenario. An upward trend in the absolute number overtreated is observable for all scenarios, largely due to population growth.

Figure 4b shows the impact that a POC diagnostic would have on reducing undertreatment, i.e. the number of women seeking care for VDS who were not given antibiotics appropriate to their infection(s). Under the treat-half and treat-most scenarios, the annual number of women undertreated for NG, CT, and TV would fall to near zero. Over 2027–2040, a POC test would lead to \>500k additional women correctly treated for NG and \~1.5m correctly treated for CT and TV (Table S12). In the treat-all scenario, undertreatment is zero in both the SOC and POC scenarios. 

Figure 4d shows how the introduction of a POC diagnostic could lead to reductions in the burden of disease by 2040\. The results here require careful interpretation: a strategy that treats more STIs without also providing prevention measures to prevent reinfection (such as partner notification/treatment, sexual health counselling, or provision of condoms) may counterintuitively lead to more new infections. However, Figure 4d shows that even in the absence of additional prevention packages for newly-diagnosed women, the reductions in undertreatment seen in Figure 4b under both the treat-half and treat-most scenarios would translate to reductions in the overall number of people infected with any of the three STIs by 2040\. In the treat-half scenario, we estimate that a POC diagnostic would reduce prevalent NG infections by 24%, CT infections by 8%, and TV infections by 7%, by 2040 (Figure 4d, Table S12). By contrast, in the treat-all scenario, the baseline standard of care has an effective sensitivity of 100%, so adding a POC diagnostic would not lead to reductions in transmission.

Treating STIs as rapidly as possible is important for minimizing onward transmission and reducing the likelihood of sequelae. In Figure 4e we show how a POC diagnostic in the context of the treat-half or treat-most syndromic management scenarios would increase the proportion of women whose STIs were resolved within 3 months. This follows as a direct result of the reduction in undertreatment shown in Figure 4b.

# Discussion 

Despite uncertainty around the implementation and performance of syndromic management, our analysis demonstrates that a POC diagnostic for NG/CT/TV would lead to 85-95% reductions in overtreatment of NG and CT across a range of assumptions around the standard of care. However, estimates of the reduction in “false negatives” (i.e., women seeking care for VDS who were not given antibiotics appropriate to their infection) are more sensitive to assumptions about the proportion of women who are treated under the current standard of care. In the treat-half scenario, a POC diagnostic could be expected to nearly eliminate missed NG, CT, and TV infections over 2027–2040. This improved sensitivity would lead to reductions of 24%, 8%, and 7% in the burden of NG, CT, and TV cases by 2040\. However, if a treat-all approach is in operation, then a POC test would not reduce infections unless other aspects of the syndromic management algorithm were also modified (e.g., a confirmed STI diagnosis may lead to improved partner notification and management). 

The optimal profile and implementation strategy for diagnostics depends on the local epidemiology of STIs. In Zimbabwe, as in many settings around the world, NG infections tend to be concentrated in high risk groups, while CT infections are found to a greater extent in lower risk groups. Both NG and CT are concentrated in younger people, whilst TV prevalence remains high in older age groups. These factors would play into determining the optimal delivery strategy for STI testing. Our analyses evaluated a multiplex assay for NG+CT+TV, but a single assay could also be beneficial, e.g. a POC diagnostic for NG could be highly impactful if packaged into HIV prevention services targeted at FSW. The impact and value proposition of a POC diagnostic would also differ if delivered to women without symptoms, e.g. as part of antenatal check-ups or sexual health care.

The analyses presented in this study have several limitations. In particular, our reported estimates of the reductions in new infections have both upside and downside risks. On the downside, our estimates may be conservative since we have not accounted for potential increases in care-seeking, treatment uptake, and partner notification and management that may be attainable if an etiological diagnosis were available. More judicious use of treatment would also mitigate supply constraints (if present), meaning that treatment would always be available to those who needed it. We have not accounted for adverse health outcomes associated with overtreatment. In particular, whilst antibiotics are used to treat BV, they can also contribute to its development or recurrence, so syndromic management of VDS may contribute to ongoing high prevalence of BV. On the upside, we have assumed complete uptake of the POC test once available, and that the healthcare worker administering the test will always treat solely on the basis of the test result. 

In addition to these limitations, there are additional data gaps that were not within scope of this study to address, but which are integral to a complete understanding of the impact of POC diagnostics for VDS. Costing data is one noteworthy gap. Another is translating estimates of reductions in new infections into estimates of broader health impact. There are a range of known adverse health outcomes associated with STIs, including HIV, preterm births, pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), tubal factor infertility, ectopic pregnancy, and eye infections. While disability adjusted life years (DALYs) have been estimated for STIs, these estimates currently only account for pelvic inflammatory disease and infertility due to NG, CT, and TV, and the data used to inform these estimates are likely to underestimate the occurrence of these conditions \[20\]. 

While our analysis models universal POC testing implementation, we recognize that real-world adoption would likely occur in phases, with better-resourced urban facilities adopting POC diagnostics before smaller clinics. Even highly accurate and affordable POC tests will fail to achieve their potential impact without adequate infrastructure for training healthcare workers, establishing supply chains, and ensuring quality assurance. Nevertheless, our approach establishes useful bounds for impact estimation. Under current practice, where no POC diagnostics are used for VDS management, we estimate 2.7-3.5 million instances of overtreatment for NG and 1.9-2.3 million instances for CT over 2027-2040 (Table S12). Universal POC testing would reduce these to 195-381K for NG and 124-233K for CT. These bounds enable interpolation for partial implementation: if POC testing were introduced at half of all clinics, overtreatment would fall to approximately 1.4-1.9M instances for NG and 1.0-1.2M for CT. This framework allows health planners to estimate impact under any level of POC coverage, supporting both phased rollout planning and resource allocation decisions. Considerable work remains to develop the delivery systems, training programs, and support mechanisms necessary to bring POC STI diagnostics to scale in resource-constrained settings. Nevertheless, our modeling results demonstrate that the potential health benefits of POC testing are substantial enough to warrant investment in addressing these implementation challenges. In addition, this current study did not consider alternative potential use cases for POC diagnostics, such as asymptomatic screening of pregnant women or high-risk populations, which would also increase their value proposition and merits further investigation.

Our analysis highlights the importance of improving understanding of the current functioning of syndromic management in practice to obtain accurate estimates of the impact and value of POC diagnostics. The range of reduction in STI transmission estimated in our model varies considerably, particularly for NG; with better data to define the standard of care, it will be possible to refine our estimates of the impact of new diagnostics and to inform possible policy and programming for the implementation of a diagnostic approach within syndromic management. Regardless of the syndromic scenario assumed, our analysis demonstrates the potential for etiologic diagnosis to substantially reduce overtreatment.  Given global concern about overtreatment and its role in AMR, these findings provide a solid basis for continued advocacy and support for POC diagnostic development.
